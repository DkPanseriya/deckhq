The characters.

A session on the floor is a person at a desk. Its colour is its state, its face is its identity, and both have to read at 22 pixels from across the room. This page has what ships today, and then the sheets two unbuilt packages are designed against — every one of those labelled as the drawing it is.

The state owns the whole body.

Six states, and they are the only thing on the floor colour is spent on. An agent can never wear a state colour as identity, and identity can never borrow one: every avatar colour is held a fixed distance from all six, checked rather than eyeballed.

  • Working Typing. Producing output right now.
  • Needs input A raised hand. Blocked on a question only you can answer.
  • Stalled Slumped. Silent longer than the stall window.
  • For review Finished its turn and standing in your office, waiting on a reply.
  • Benched You have dealt with it. In the lounge, out of the queue.
  • Let go Gone from disk. On its way off the floor.

The names are the model, not the colour: a state is carried by a pose and a label as well as by a hue, so the floor is legible to a reader who sees neither. The model in 60 seconds is the whole of it.

What ships today.

Identity is a face, a name and sometimes a hat, derived from the session id, so the same session is the same person on every restart. The name comes from a pool of 600 and is kept for good.

Reduced motion is a first-class render, not a degradation. Under prefers-reduced-motion characters snap instead of walking, each clip holds one representative pose, and the floor stays fully legible — with a golden proving it.

How that figure was chosen.

Four candidates were drawn as full sheets — sizes, states, identity, in situ — and put on the same lounge crop at 1:1, 34 pixels tall, because the argument is only settled at the size the floor actually draws.

B won: a 45° three-quarter robot, symmetric and front-facing, so all six poses read from any scan direction, and the bright visor is the most findable element on the floor.

Four candidates, at actual size.

All four on one lounge crop of a real floor, 34 pixels tall. This comparison is what settled it, and it is the only fair way to settle it.

The sheets on this page are the proposal, not the product. Every golden in the repository changed when the winner landed.

Coming in WP-87: the ways it moves.

The figure can stand six ways and move none. WP-87 gives it a thinking cloud, a working clip, a run cycle, and the lounge activities a benched agent has nothing to do without. None of it is built. The two sheets below are what it is designed against; they are rendered by scripts in this repository from the same drawing code the floor uses, which makes them faithful and does not make them screenshots.

What already binds it: one static frame per state under prefers-reduced-motion with a golden proving it, and every phase from the injected clock rather than the wall clock, so a capture of a moving floor is reproducible.

Coming in WP-89: the crew.

A session that spawns sub-agents should show them. WP-89 draws three or more live sub-agents smaller on the floor around their parent, each with a laptop, joined by cables carrying data pulses back toward the parent desk. Not built. Today sub-agents are on the floor as juniors beside their parent, without the cables.

The mark

DeckHQ’s own icon lives once, as a hand-written SVG — six rounded rectangles, both grounds in one file, no font and no external reference — and every PNG, the favicon.ico and the VS Code tile are rendered from it by a script that rasterises the shapes itself rather than driving a browser. A test compares the committed files against a fresh render on every run, so the source and its outputs cannot drift apart.

The one colour in it is the amber on the antenna tip, and it is the accent this site is built on.
