Ten things the floor does.

Every picture below is a crop of DeckHQ running, taken from the demo fixture on a pinned clock by one manifest and one script. Each one shows the part the words are about, at the size it is drawn.

Your projects, as an office.

Every project on disk with a live or recent session is a room, and the people in it are that project’s sessions. The building is generated from the population: the same nine sessions produce the same floor every time, and no room is drawn for a repository nobody is working in.

Typing means working. A raised hand means blocked on a question. Slumped means silent longer than the stall window. The six states are the whole model, and they are the only thing on the floor colour is spent on.

A room is a repository.

The plate carries the project name, how many sessions are in it, how many tokens they have spent today, and whether the cache is working. Sub-agents sit beside their parent as juniors, named after it.

Room size follows session count, so a repository with four people in it is visibly the busy one. The packer lays the rooms out fresh on every resize.

They wait in your office.

A session that finishes its turn walks out of its room and into yours. It sits on a sofa with a badge over its head saying how long it has been there, and the oldest one is the one you owe.

The header counts them. Clearing the office is the whole loop: reply, approve or bench, and the room fills up again.

A desk says what a session is doing.

Working is typing at the screen. Thinking is a cloud over the head. Both run off one injected clock, so every animation on the floor agrees about what time it is and a screenshot of a moving floor can be taken twice and come out the same.

Under prefers-reduced-motion each clip holds one representative pose, and a golden proves the floor still reads.

The strip says who is waiting.

A row of chips under the header carries the queue without leaving the floor: oldest on the left, and it stays there. Each chip is the wait, the name and the project.

Tab swaps the floor for the deck — the same queue as a semantic table, with the same actions, so a screen reader reaches everything the floor shows. The same queue is in your terminal as deckhq waiting.

The panel opens on the review.

Click anyone and the review material is already in front of you: how long they have waited, what they said rendered as the markdown they wrote, and what changed in that project’s working tree, read from git.

Approve is a send, never an acknowledgement. It posts the reply exactly as typing it would, and the review is discharged when the runtime records your turn. The heading over the diff names the project, because several agents can share one repository.

Answer a permission from the panel.

When a session stops to ask before it runs something, a card appears above what it said with the tool and its literal input. Allow and Deny are on A and D. The terminal prompt stays live the whole time, so a closed DeckHQ can never block anybody.

This has been proven once — Claude Code 2.1.260 on Windows, 4 September 2026, one real session, two real prompts. Codex, Gemini CLI and OpenCode cannot be answered from the panel. Adapters has the rest.

Tokens, by project and by session.

Tab → Usage gives you a window — today, 7 days, 30 days — the total, and the split into input, cache write, cache read and output, with tables for where it went: by project, by session, by model, by day and by tool.

Dollars are one setting away and off by default. Turn on Show cost and the list-price estimate appears everywhere, carrying the dated rate card it came from. It is an estimate and never a bill.

Repositories nobody is working in.

A room is drawn for a project somebody is working in. The rest are one quiet chip in the corner, and I opens the list behind it.

Pinning one keeps it a room with nothing running in it: a small room, one desk, nobody at it, and a plate reading pinned. The pin lives in state.json and nothing your agents do can clear it.

Benched sessions go to the lounge.

Bench a session and it leaves your office for the lounge, out of the queue and off the count. It is still on disk and still resumable; it is no longer something you owe.

The lounge has four bays and the benched fill them. Twelve animations ship, so the room you are not being asked for anything by still moves.

And in the terminal

  • deckhq waiting

    The whole queue, printed: who is waiting, on what project, for how long.

  • deckhq ack <id>

    Discharges one. Reading works with or without a daemon; acting needs one.

  • deckhq statusline

    ▣ 3 waiting · 1 hand up for a status bar, in every terminal you have open.

  • deckhq stats

    Replays the local ledger: tokens by project, by session, by model, by day.

  • deckhq doctor

    The environment report. Ten seconds, sends nothing, writes nothing, starts nothing.

And around the floor

  • App mode

    deckhq app opens the floor in Chrome or Edge as an application: no tab strip, no address bar, its own taskbar button, and its own profile under ~/.deckhq/app-profile, so the window keeps its size and never shares your tabs. The title carries the needs-you count. Run on Windows; the macOS and Linux files are written from the platform documentation and have not been executed.

  • Notifications

    Two events are worth an interruption: a hand going up, and a working session whose process goes away without its runtime saying goodbye. Finished-and-waiting is a count you consult, never a toast.

  • Sound

    Three cues exist — a hand up, a turn finishing, the office cleared — and sound ships off. None of them plays until you turn it on in Settings.

  • Themes

    Three finishes, all free: the warm office, night shift and blueprint. A theme repaints materials and neutrals; the six state colours never move. The look has all three side by side.
