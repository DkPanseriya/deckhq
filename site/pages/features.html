Nine things the floor does.

One picture each, and every picture says what it is. A golden render is the renderer’s own output, re-checked pixel for pixel on every CI run; a capture is DeckHQ photographed on a real machine. Several of the captures here are from September 2026 and show the figures as they were before WP-79 redrew them — docs/MEDIA.md lists every one for recapture.

The floor, and who sits where.

Every project on disk with a live or recent session is a room, and the people in it are that project’s sessions. The floor is generated from the population, not from a saved layout: the same nine sessions produce the same building every time, and a room is never drawn for a repository nobody is working in.

Typing means working. A raised hand means blocked on a question. Slumped means silent longer than the stall window. The six states are the whole model, and they are the only thing on the floor colour is spent on.

The queue, and the deck.

A strip of chips under the header carries the queue’s shape without leaving the floor: oldest on the left, and it stays there. Tab swaps the floor for the deck — the same queue, the same order, the same actions, as a table.

The deck is a real semantic table, so a screen reader reaches everything the floor shows and the floor is never the only way to get to anything. The same queue is in your terminal as deckhq waiting.

The panel, and the review card.

Click anyone and the panel opens beside the floor with the review material already in front of you: how long they have been waiting, what they said rendered as the markdown they actually wrote, and what changed in that project’s working tree, read straight from git.

Approve is a send, never an acknowledgement. It posts the reply exactly as typing it would, and the review is discharged when the runtime records your turn. The heading over the diff names the project, never the agent, because several agents can share one repository.

The permission card.

When a session stops to ask before it runs something, you can answer from the panel: a card appears above what it said with the tool and its literal input, and Allow / Deny on A / D. The terminal prompt stays live the whole time, so a closed DeckHQ can never block anybody.

This has been proven once — Claude Code 2.1.260 on Windows, 4 September 2026, one real session, two real prompts. Codex, Gemini CLI and OpenCode cannot be answered from the panel at all. Adapters has the rest.

Token usage.

Tab → Usage gives you a window — today, 7 days, 30 days — the total, and the split into input, cache write, cache read and output, with tables for where it went: by project, by session, by model, by day and by tool.

Dollars are one setting away and off by default. Turn on Show cost and the list-price estimate appears everywhere, always carrying the dated rate card it came from. It is an estimate and never a bill.

Pinning, and the popover.

A room is drawn for a project somebody is working in. The repositories nobody is working in are a quiet chip in the corner of the floor, and I opens the list behind it.

Pinning one keeps it a room even with nothing running in it: a small room, one desk, nobody at it, and a plate that says pinned. The pin lives in state.json and nothing your agents do can clear it.

App mode.

deckhq app opens the floor in Chrome or Edge in application mode: no tab strip, no address bar, its own taskbar button, and its own browser profile under ~/.deckhq/app-profile, so the window keeps its size and position and never shares your tabs or your extensions.

The title carries the needs-you count, so the taskbar is a live badge for the queue. Windows is the platform this was run on; the macOS and Linux files are written from Apple’s and freedesktop.org’s documentation and have never been executed.

Notifications, and sound.

Two events are worth an interruption and no more: an agent raising its hand, and a working session whose process goes away without its runtime saying goodbye. Finished-and-waiting and stalled are a count you consult when you choose to, never a toast.

Sound ships off. Three cues exist — a hand going up, a turn finishing, and the office being cleared — and none of them plays until you ask for it in Settings. Verified on Windows; the macOS and Linux commands are asserted in the test suite and have not been run on those platforms.

Themes.

Three finishes, all free and none of them gated: the default warm office, night shift — the same office after hours, cooler and dimmer — and blueprint, the floor as a drawing on a drafting table.

A theme repaints materials and neutrals and nothing else. The six state colours never move, so a raised hand is the same amber in every theme, and every theme is measured against the same contrast floors before it can be selected. The Look page has all three side by side.

And in the terminal

  • deckhq waiting

    The whole queue, printed: who is waiting, on what project, for how long.

  • deckhq ack <id>

    Discharges one. Reading works with or without a daemon; acting needs one.

  • deckhq statusline

    ▣ 3 waiting · 1 hand up for a status bar, in every terminal you have open.

  • deckhq stats

    Replays the local ledger: tokens by project, by session, by model, by day.

  • deckhq doctor

    The environment report. Ten seconds, sends nothing, writes nothing, starts nothing.
