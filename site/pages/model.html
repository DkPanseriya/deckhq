The model in 60 seconds

An office is only worth having if it tells the truth about who is waiting on you. Everything below follows from that.

The one rule

What you owe is decided by you, never by the runtime.

A session has two kinds of state, and DeckHQ never lets one overwrite the other. activityState is observed: it changes on its own as the session starts, produces output, blocks on a question, goes quiet, exits. ackState is yours: it changes only when you act.

The waiting area in your office renders your acknowledgement, not the runtime’s opinion. Opening a conversation does not clear it. Scrolling past it does not clear it. Reading it does not clear it. Only an explicit action does.

There are named INVARIANT: tests for this. No observed event may clear a user-owned state, and a change that needs those tests relaxed is the wrong change.

The six states

State What it means Where the agent is What you see
working Live and producing output Its project desk Typing, occasional coffee
needs_input Live, blocked on a question or a permission prompt Stays at its desk Raises a hand, pulsing ring
stalled Live but silent longer than the stall window Its desk Slumped, amber
for_review Finished a turn, waiting on you Walks to your office Standing in the waiting area with a waiting-time badge
benched Reviewed, no work assigned, available The lounge Pool, table tennis, arcade, coffee
let_go Off the floor Hidden Reachable from ⌘K → “Show let-go agents”

working, needs_input, stalled and for_review are observed. benched and let_go are yours. for_review is entered automatically and can only be left by you — that last sentence is the invariant, in one line.

Why two “needs you” signals

A raised hand at a desk means I am mid-task and blocked. A person standing in your office means I finished; review this. Those need different responses from you, so they look different and are counted separately. The header carries both numbers rather than one total that hides which is which.

Where an agent lives

A session belongs to a project, decided by its working directory, and every session in a project sits at that project’s tables whether or not it is running. An agent leaves its room in exactly two ways: it finishes a turn and walks to your office, or you bench it and it goes to the lounge to rest until you recall it.

Rooms exist because people are in them. A project with no sessions has no room, and one appears the moment work starts there — the floor is generated from the people on it, not from the repositories on your disk.

The lounge is not filler

A benched agent is available capacity, and the product makes that read as a good thing rather than as waste. Watching them enjoy themselves is what a cleared queue looks like.

What is never scored

The agents are the characters. You are the manager, and the manager is never scored: no streaks, no levels, no badges, no guilt message about a queue you have not cleared. Agents have names, faces and histories; you have a queue and a way to empty it.

Cost is an estimate, never a bill. DeckHQ multiplies observed token counts by public list prices so you can compare projects against each other. It has no idea what your plan actually charges you, and it says so everywhere the number appears.
